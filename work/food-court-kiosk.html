B2B self-ordering kiosk, food courts

Self-ordering kiosk for food courts

A self-ordering kiosk platform that restaurant brands buy for their food courts. I redesigned the whole ordering flow, from choosing items to collecting the receipt, and turned the last screen from one people walked away from into one they act on.

Role
End-to-end UX/UI redesign
Scope
Items, add-ons, payment, receipts
Model
B2B, one platform for many restaurant brands

One kiosk, many counters

In a food court, the kiosk isn’t one restaurant’s till. It takes orders for several small shops at once: a juice counter, a tea stall, a meals counter, often standing side by side.

Each shop needs its own receipt. It’s the customer’s token at that counter, the proof of what they ordered and paid for.

Before

An arrow wasn’t enough

On the kiosk, the screen sits at eye level and the receipt printer sits well below it, near waist height. While people order, they look at the screen. The slot is out of their line of sight.

The first success screen showed a down arrow pointing at that slot, and nothing else. It assumed people would look down, and that they knew a receipt was coming and needed it. They didn’t. They paid, saw the screen and walked away, and the receipt stayed in the slot.

In a food court that receipt is the token you hand over to collect your food. A receipt left in the slot means a customer at the counter with no proof of what they paid for. And when one order covers several shops, the second and third slips come out after the first, which makes them even easier to miss.

After

Show the receipt printing, then point at it

I replaced the bare arrow with an animation of the receipt printing, and pointed the arrow down to the slot.

The receipt on screen tells people what to look for; the arrow tells them where. People recognise a receipt the moment they see one coming out, so they looked down, found it and picked it up without stopping to work out what to do.

  1. Show the receipt, not just the slot

    The screen animates a receipt printing in step with the real printer, so people see what they’re waiting for before it arrives.

  2. Lead the eye down

    The slot is out of view, so the arrow bridges the gap: from the receipt printing on screen down to the real one near waist height.

  3. Count every slip

    When an order covers several shops, a running count, 1 of 3, 2 of 3, tells people how many to expect and gives them a reason to wait for the last one.

  4. Say done only when it’s done

    The screen changes to its finished state when the count reaches the total, then tells people where each receipt goes.

I redesigned the whole order, end to end

The receipt screen is the part people remember, but I redesigned every step before it too, from the first item to the last receipt.

  1. Select items

    Browse the menus and add items to the order.

  2. Add-ons

    Pick extras and options for each item.

  3. Payment method

    Choose to pay by card or UPI.

  4. Pay

    Complete the payment by card or UPI.

  5. Success & receipt

    Payment confirmed, receipt printing on screen, arrow to the slot.

One platform, many restaurant brands

The kiosk is sold business to business. Each restaurant brand that buys it brings its own shops and menus, and the same screens have to serve all of them.

So I designed the flow as a platform rather than a one-off: one set of screens that takes any brand’s content.

  • Any brand’s menu

    Shops, items and add-ons load into the same layouts, whichever restaurant runs the kiosk.

  • One shop or a whole food court

    The receipt step works the same for a single receipt or one per shop.

  • One flow for every client

    Every brand gets the same ordering, payment and receipt flow, so an improvement made once reaches all of them.

An arrow points at a slot. A receipt printing on screen tells people what’s coming, and that it’s theirs to take.
